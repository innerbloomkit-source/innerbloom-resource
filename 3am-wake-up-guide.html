Why This Happens

Two things working against your sleep.

Progesterone does more than most people realise. It is not only a reproductive hormone. It is part of how your body holds you in deep, continuous sleep — through a calming system in the brain that acts like a self-produced sedative.

When progesterone is stable, that calm holds. When it starts fluctuating in perimenopause, the support becomes inconsistent. The brain becomes more easily activated at night. Light sleep that would normally pass unnoticed can tip into full wakefulness.

At the same time, cortisol — your stress hormone — has a natural rhythm. It begins rising in the second half of the night, before you wake. For a woman whose sleep is already fragile from progesterone shifts, that early cortisol rise can be the thing that pulls her fully awake, hours too soon.

Worth Trying

What may actually help.

Not fixes. Starting points — things worth testing for a couple of weeks to see if they change your pattern.

Tracking the pattern

Symptoms feel random until the pattern becomes visible. Progesterone tends to be lowest in the premenstrual phase and in cycles where ovulation did not occur. If your wake-ups cluster at specific points, that is information — not to judge, just to notice.

Reducing evening cortisol load

Cortisol is cumulative. A high-stress evening can extend into the early morning. Anything that genuinely lowers stress in the two to three hours before sleep may help — a walk, quiet time, less stimulation. Not as a rule. As an experiment.

Blood sugar stability before bed

A cortisol rise can be amplified by blood sugar dropping overnight. A small protein-based snack before bed — not a meal, something small — may help stabilise it for some women. Worth testing for two weeks to see if it shifts the pattern.

A doctor who understands perimenopause

If this pattern is consistent and significantly affecting your life, it deserves a proper conversation — not general sleep medicine, but a professional who specifically understands perimenopausal hormone fluctuation. This is a legitimate pattern. It deserves to be taken seriously.
